### Why this is a constraint

To model the route, we typically introduce binary decision variables:

\[
x_{i,j} =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if the traveler goes directly from city } i \text{ to city } j \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\]

To ensure that:

- Every city is **entered exactly once**, and  
- Every city is **left exactly once**,

\[
\sum_{j \ne i} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall i
\]
(outgoing degree constraint)

\[
\sum_{i \ne j} x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall j
\]
(incoming degree constraint)

These are essential to ensure that:

- Each city has exactly one successor,
- Each city has exactly one predecessor.

Without these constraints, the model would not represent a valid tour structure at all.

\end{cases}
\]

If we do **not** explicitly forbid \( x_{i,i} \), then:

- The degree constraints  
  \[
  \sum_j x_{i,j} = 1 \quad \forall i
  \]
  \[

- A self-loop satisfies both the inbound and outbound degree constraints for that city.
- This creates a degenerate subtour of length 1.
- If the diagonal distances are zero (which is common), the solver may exploit this.

Therefore, unless:

- You explicitly exclude variables where \( i = j \), **or**
- You enforce \( x_{i,i} = 0 \),

the formulation is incorrect.
